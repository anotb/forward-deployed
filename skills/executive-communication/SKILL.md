# Executive communication

Help the audience see what AI changes and decide what to do about it. Build the argument around their business, the work people will delegate and the commitment in front of them. Deliver the finished memo, deck, walkthrough or interactive explanation requested.

## Make a consequential argument

Lead with the recommendation or insight. Explain the mechanism: what AI will accomplish, how people will work differently, and why that creates value. An adoption proposal might let commercial teams investigate customer economics themselves, or let engineers delegate a migration across a codebase. Give the audience a specific change they can imagine using.

Connect ambition to demonstrated capability. Show the AI pursuing an assignment: obtaining context, using tools, resolving a conflict, creating an artifact and responding to new information. An initial deployment should exercise the part that makes the larger idea valuable. Explain what becomes possible if that works across the relevant team or business population.

Make the economics follow the changed work. Count preparation, review and coordination together where effort moves between roles. Distinguish released capacity, faster decisions, wider coverage and cash savings; choose the benefit the deployment can support. A prototype or illustrative scenario can explain the mechanism without being presented as a customer result.

Address the strongest objection where it affects the choice. If the new workflow consumes scarce expert attention, show how the design uses that attention and whether the resulting service is worth it. If access or operating ownership limits expansion, propose a deployment that resolves that constraint. Change the recommendation when the evidence warrants it.

For continuing engagements, explain what changed, which commitment it affects and the feasible response. Keep an estimate, an accepted date and a recommendation distinct. State the choice directly without diluting every sentence with "potential," "proposed" or "subject to validation."

## Show the work clearly

Choose exhibits that make the argument visible. A before-and-after assignment can show how a role expands. A sequence can show an agent revisiting a conclusion after fresh evidence. A workload comparison can expose where effort moves. An architecture view can explain how the AI works with existing systems and who controls consequential actions.

Use a recognizable operating episode to connect those views. Show enough of the source, AI work and finished result for the audience to judge the capability. Label an illustrative episode once, clearly. Keep detailed source locators and technical implementation in notes or backup where they support scrutiny without interrupting the argument.

Adapt to the decision maker. A sponsor needs the business choice, resource commitment and path to broader value. A team leader needs to see the changed work and support burden. Engineers need a credible account of context, tools, state and integration. Use a shared story with the depth each audience needs.

## Finish the artifact

Use direct language and finding-led titles. Replace generic transformation claims with the specific assignment, capability and consequence. Make the requested decision, your recommendation and its rationale easy to find. A funding amount or delivery date belongs in the artifact when supported by the underlying plan.

Use the host's authoring capabilities and the requested template. Build explanatory graphics and editable objects where the audience will revise the work. Inspect the finished sequence, figures, legibility and layout in its intended form. Deliver the usable artifact with a brief statement of purpose and any material delivery limitation; keep authoring logs out of it.
